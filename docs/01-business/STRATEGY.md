# Business Strategy — LYSHEIM

> Consulted hat: **Senior Business Strategist**. Owner: Founder.
> **Framing:** this is a *simulated* business used as a portfolio artifact. The analysis is
> done as if the store were real — with explicit assumptions and confidence levels — but it
> is **decision-support, not financial or legal advice**, and no outcome is guaranteed.
> Figures are third-party estimates and are directional.

---

## Context

- **Business:** direct-to-consumer e-commerce store selling household goods and home
  furniture (an "IKEA-inspired" category and brand feel).
- **Primary market:** **international, English-first, priced in USD.** Lead regions are
  **North America and Western Europe**, where online furniture penetration and D2C appetite
  are highest; **Asia-Pacific** is the fastest-growing secondary region and a later target.
- **Strategic question:** *Which business model should a solo founder with strong
  engineering (backend + data) but no retail or supply-chain capability choose, so that the
  store is both credible as a business and finishable as a portfolio project?*
- **Constraints:** one person; no capital for inventory or paid acquisition; no brand,
  supply-chain, or logistics capability; must ship a polished, deployed demo. The founder is
  based in Indonesia, but the model is deliberately **asset-light and region-agnostic**.

## Strategic Analysis

> Evidence is drawn from public market-research summaries (Mordor Intelligence, Expert
> Market Research, Baymard Institute, Statista, The Business Research Company, Market
> Research Future). Figures are third-party **estimates**. Confidence reflects agreement
> across sources, not certainty.

- [ ] **STRAT-ANALYSIS-1.1 [Market size & growth]**
  - **Finding:** The global **online furniture** market is ~**USD 131.4B (2025)**, heading
    to ~**USD 202.3B by 2031** (≈**7.7% CAGR**). The broader **furniture & home-goods**
    market is ~**USD 609B (2025)**. Global retail e-commerce is ~**USD 7.4T (2026)**,
    growing ~8% year on year.
  - **Evidence:** Mordor Intelligence (online furniture); Expert Market Research (furniture
    & home goods, 5.6% CAGR to 2035); SellersCommerce / Statista (retail e-commerce).
  - **Implication:** The category is large and steadily growing; most near-term growth comes
    from the shift of purchases online rather than category expansion.
  - **Confidence:** Medium.

- [ ] **STRAT-ANALYSIS-1.2 [Channel shift — and the marketplace threat]**
  - **Finding:** Online is ~**21% of retail (2025)** and rising, but **marketplaces**
    (Amazon, Wayfair, regional players) capture a large share of online home-goods demand.
    The Furniture & Home e-commerce segment is growing at ~**10.8% CAGR**, explicitly
    attributed to **AR visualization and D2C disruption**.
  - **Evidence:** Statista (online retail share); Market Research Future (segment CAGR and
    drivers).
  - **Implication:** A standalone store cannot win on selection or price against
    marketplaces. It must win on **brand, curation, and digital experience** — the levers a
    strong engineer actually controls.
  - **Confidence:** Medium.

- [ ] **STRAT-ANALYSIS-1.3 [Conversion economics are brutal — experience is the battleground]**
  - **Finding:** The global average **cart-abandonment rate is ~70.2%** and has barely moved
    in a decade. **Home & furniture runs markedly higher, ~79–80%.**
  - **Evidence:** Baymard Institute (70.22%, meta-analysis of ~50 studies); Upcounting
    (78.77%), Geysera (80.3%), Primer (79.1%) for home & furniture.
  - **Implication:** Roughly **8 in 10 carts are abandoned** in this category, because it is
    a considered, big-ticket purchase. The winning levers are **trust, shipping-cost
    transparency, and checkout UX** — precisely what a well-engineered store controls. This
    is the strongest justification for investing in engineering quality.
  - **Confidence:** Medium-High.

- [ ] **STRAT-ANALYSIS-1.4 [Category & price structure]**
  - **Finding:** Within online furniture, **home furniture dominates (~68%)**. Demand is
    shifting toward **multifunctional, versatile, and modular** pieces suited to smaller
    living spaces. The **mid-range** price band is the largest and most brand-sensitive.
  - **Evidence:** Mordor Intelligence (home furniture share); D2C trend reports.
  - **Implication:** Position **mid-range**, lead with **living/dining and storage**, and lean
    into a "designed but affordable" story.
  - **Confidence:** Medium.

- [ ] **STRAT-ANALYSIS-1.5 [Competitive landscape]**
  - **Finding:** The field splits into (a) *mass / omnichannel* — **IKEA, Wayfair, Amazon**;
    (b) *online-first D2C* — **Article, Burrow, Floyd**, with cautionary tales such as
    **Made.com** (collapsed 2022, a unit-economics failure); and (c) *marketplaces*. North
    America leads online furniture at ~**36%** of the global market.
  - **Evidence:** market reports; press coverage of Made.com's collapse.
  - **Implication:** Incumbents own brand and traffic. The defensible white space for a new
    entrant is a **focused, design-led catalog with a clearly better digital experience** —
    not a broad catalog.
  - **Confidence:** Medium.

- [ ] **STRAT-ANALYSIS-1.6 [Internal capability gap]**
  - **Finding:** The founder has **strong software and data-engineering capability** but
    **no supply-chain, brand, or paid-marketing capability**.
  - **Evidence:** Founder background (IT since 2017; backend and data engineering).
  - **Implication:** Any model requiring inventory or warehousing is a poor fit. The model
    must be **asset-light** and play directly to engineering strengths.
  - **Confidence:** High.

### Porter's Five Forces (quick pass)

| Force | Intensity | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Rivalry | High | Fragmented; marketplaces, incumbents, and thousands of small sellers. |
| Supplier power | Medium | Many furniture suppliers; differentiation comes from curation, not supply. |
| Buyer power | High | Low switching cost; price transparency everywhere. |
| Substitutes | Medium | Second-hand/refurbished, DIY, marketplace alternatives. |
| New entrants | High | Low online barriers; the moat is brand and experience, not capital. |

**Net:** structurally unattractive on price — which is exactly why the strategy must
compete on **brand and experience**, not cost.

### Customer segmentation

- [ ] **SEG-A [Urban renters / first-home, 25–35] — primary target**
  - Needs: furnish a small apartment affordably; small-footprint, multi-function pieces;
    fast, transparent delivery.
  - Willingness to pay: mid-range; highly price- and trust-sensitive.
- [ ] **SEG-B [Young families upgrading, 30–40]**
  - Needs: living/dining furniture, durability, safe materials.
  - Willingness to pay: mid-range to upper-mid.
- [ ] **SEG-C (secondary) [Remote workers fitting a home office]**
  - Needs: desks, chairs, and storage on a modest budget.

**Primary target:** **SEG-A** — large, underserved on *small-space* curation, and the
natural fit for a design-led brand.

## Strategic Options

- [ ] **STRAT-OPTION-1.1 [A — Curated asset-light D2C brand ("IKEA-lite")]**
  - **Description:** A single-brand store with a *curated* mid-range catalog. Assume
    dropship/consignment from regional suppliers so no inventory is held; build a strong
    brand and a best-in-class digital experience.
  - **Rationale:** Wins on brand and experience (the defensible levers); asset-light; directly
    showcases engineering; finishable by one person.
  - **Trade-offs:** Thin margins early; depends on suppliers; must generate its own traffic.
    **Reversible** — catalog and positioning can change cheaply.
  - **Fit:** Excellent (plays to engineering and data strengths; avoids the supply-chain gap).

- [ ] **STRAT-OPTION-1.2 [B — Multi-vendor marketplace]**
  - **Description:** Onboard many sellers; the store is the platform.
  - **Rationale:** Scales selection without inventory.
  - **Trade-offs:** Needs seller onboarding, payouts, disputes, trust and safety — an
    enormous operational surface. **Not finishable** by one person for a portfolio.
  - **Fit:** Poor.

- [ ] **STRAT-OPTION-1.3 [C — Marketplace-native seller (no own storefront)]**
  - **Description:** Sell exclusively through Amazon/Wayfair rather than a standalone site.
  - **Rationale:** Meets customers where they are; lowest traffic cost.
  - **Trade-offs:** No brand ownership and no portfolio artifact (the point of the project).
  - **Fit:** Poor.

- [ ] **STRAT-OPTION-1.4 [D — Niche: small-space, modular home goods]**
  - **Description:** Own the "small apartment, smart storage" niche with a tightly curated
    range of modular and multi-function pieces.
  - **Rationale:** Defensible through focus; clear positioning; matches SEG-A and the
    modular-product trend.
  - **Trade-offs:** Smaller TAM; needs strong content and community.
  - **Fit:** Strong — and it *combines* with Option A.

## Recommendation

**Recommended path: Option A + Option D — a curated, asset-light, D2C home-goods brand
focused on small-space, modular living ("IKEA-lite, small-space-first").**

- **Why:** It is the only option that simultaneously (a) is defensible against marketplaces
  through brand and experience, (b) is finishable by a solo founder, (c) avoids the
  supply-chain capability gap via dropship/consignment, and (d) is an excellent showcase of
  software and data engineering.
- **Trade-offs accepted:** slower revenue ramp, thin early margins, and a real dependency on
  the founder generating traffic and building a brand.
- **Reversibility:** High — the catalog and channel can change cheaply; the codebase is
  reusable in every scenario.
- **Kill criteria (what would prove this wrong):** if the store cannot sustain **> ~35%
  gross margin after shipping**, or if landing-page→add-to-cart stays under **~5%** after
  meaningful experience investment, the model is not working.

### Unit economics — illustrative only

> Numbers are **illustrative placeholders** with stated assumptions; they show the *shape*
> of the economics, not a forecast. Confidence: **Low**.

| Metric | Illustrative value | Assumption |
|---|---|---|
| Average order value (AOV) | ~USD 180 | Mid-range furniture item plus an accessory |
| COGS | 55–60% of AOV | Dropship/wholesale cost |
| Shipping | ~near cost | Charged to customer; thin margin |
| **Gross margin** | **~40–45%** | AOV − COGS; before shipping ops |
| CAC (paid) | USD 60–140 | Meta/TikTok ads, international benchmarks |
| Contribution margin | positive only if CAC < ~USD 120 | The central pressure point |
| LTV (12-month, repeat) | 1.3–1.6× first order | Optimistic; requires retention work |

**Read:** the model only works with **cheap organic traffic** (content, SEO, social) or
strong **repeat/AOV** — which is why engineering quality and the (later) data track matter:
they are the levers that reduce CAC and lift conversion.

### Positioning & differentiation

- **For** urban renters and young families in North America and Western Europe **who** need
  affordable, well-designed, small-space home goods,
- **LYSHEIM** is a **design-led home-goods store** that offers a **curated, modular,
  small-space-first** catalog with a **transparent, fast, trustworthy buying experience**,
- **unlike** IKEA/Wayfair (big-box, broad, showroom- or marketplace-centric) and the
  marketplaces (endless, unfiltered, low-trust),
- **because** we compete on **curation and experience**, not on breadth or price.

**Defensibility:** brand + curation + experience (hard to copy quickly), later reinforced by
a data-driven personalization and retention loop.
